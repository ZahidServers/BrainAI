★ CHAPTER G11PS-CORE — Political Theory: Freedom, Equality, Rights, Justice — FULLER ANCHOR
1. METADATA
• Grade 11 · NCERT · Humanities · Political Science · Unit: Political Theory · Prereq: basic civics (Grade 9–10) · Difficulty: Medium · Objectives: define politics and political theory; understand freedom (negative/positive), equality (kinds), rights (why & which), and justice (distributive) as reasoned concepts, not slogans; connect them to the Indian Constitution.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL
(a) What is politics & political theory? Politics is the activity through which people make, preserve and amend the general rules under which they live — involving negotiation, power, and the public good. Political theory examines the ideas and principles (freedom, equality, justice, rights, democracy) that underlie political life, clarifying their meaning and defending or criticising them with arguments.
(b) Freedom (liberty). Freedom is the absence of constraints and the presence of conditions for self-development.
 • Negative liberty — freedom from external interference (a protected private sphere).
 • Positive liberty — freedom to develop one's potential, needing enabling conditions (education, health).
 • Harm principle (J.S. Mill): a person's liberty may be restricted only to prevent harm to others; "self-regarding" acts should be free.
(c) Equality. The claim that all humans are of equal worth and deserve equal consideration.
 • Natural vs social inequalities: some differences (talent) vs those created by society (caste, class) — the latter are the concern of justice.
 • Kinds: political (equal vote/rights), economic (opportunity, reducing wealth gaps), social (dignity, non-discrimination).
 • Equality ≠ sameness: it means equal treatment of equals and, sometimes, differential treatment (affirmative action) to achieve real equality of opportunity.
(d) Rights. Justified claims of individuals, recognised by society/state, essential for a life of dignity.
 • Why rights? They protect vital human interests (life, liberty, expression) and set limits on state power.
 • Kinds: political, economic, cultural; legal rights vs human/moral rights. Rights carry duties (my right to free speech implies others' duty not to silence me).
(e) Justice. Giving each their due. Distributive justice concerns the fair distribution of benefits and burdens. Rawls' "veil of ignorance": just principles are those we would choose if we didn't know our own place in society — leading to protecting the least advantaged.
(f) Link to the Indian Constitution. These concepts are embedded as Fundamental Rights (equality, freedom, against exploitation, religion, cultural, remedies), Directive Principles (social/economic justice goals), and the Preamble (justice, liberty, equality, fraternity).

FACT vs INTERPRETATION vs OPINION.
 • FACT: The Indian Constitution guarantees the right to equality (Articles 14–18).
 • INTERPRETATION: Scholars argue reservations are a means to substantive equality.
 • OPINION: "Complete economic equality is the ideal society" — a value judgement.

A. DEFINITIONS (10)
politics, political theory, negative liberty, positive liberty, harm principle, equality, distributive justice, rights, duty, veil of ignorance.

B. QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
Easy: What is negative liberty? (Freedom from interference.) Which principle limits liberty to prevent harm? (Harm principle.) Medium: Distinguish natural and social inequalities. Why do rights imply duties? (Others must respect them.) Difficult: Explain Rawls' veil of ignorance. Application: Is affirmative action consistent with equality? (Yes — it aims at real equality of opportunity.) Reasoning: Why is "equality" not the same as "sameness"? (Equals treated equally; unequal starting points may need differential support.)

F/G. SHORT/LONG
Short: Differentiate negative and positive liberty with an example. Long (model): "Discuss whether affirmative action is compatible with equality." Model — Formal equality (identical treatment) can perpetuate disadvantage when starting points differ. Substantive equality aims at genuine equality of opportunity; affirmative action (e.g., reservations) temporarily gives differential support to historically disadvantaged groups to level the field. Critics interpret it as reverse discrimination; defenders see it as required by real equality. (Present as reasoned positions; the conclusion is contested — an interpretive debate, not a settled fact.)

H. HOTS
Can a society be free but unequal, or equal but unfree? (Yes — liberty and equality can tension; theories balance them differently.) Why might unlimited freedom threaten equality? (The powerful could dominate the weak absent limits.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS
"Freedom means doing anything" → it's constrained by others' rights/harm. "Equality means everyone is identical" → equal worth/consideration, not sameness. "Rights are unlimited" → they carry duties and reasonable restrictions.

J. REVISION

===== SOCIOLOGY =====

★ CHAPTER G11SO-FOUND — Foundational Sociological Concepts — FULLER ANCHOR
METADATA. Grade 11 · NCERT · Sociology · Prereq: none · Difficulty: Medium · Objectives: define sociology & its perspective; understand society, social structure, socialisation, social institutions, status/role, culture; distinguish sociology from common sense.
TEACHING.
• What is sociology? The systematic study of human society and social behaviour — how groups, institutions and structures shape individuals and vice versa. The sociological perspective ("imagination", C. Wright Mills) links personal troubles to public issues (e.g., one person's unemployment vs a society-wide recession). It differs from common sense by using evidence, theory and method.
• Society & social structure. Society is an organised web of social relationships; social structure is the relatively stable patterning of these relationships (institutions, groups, roles) that persists beyond individuals.
• Socialisation. The lifelong process by which individuals learn the norms, values and roles of their society — via agents: family (primary), school, peer group, media, workplace. It makes culture continue across generations.
• Status & role. A status is a social position (student, mother); a role is the expected behaviour attached to it. Ascribed status (given by birth, e.g., caste, sex) vs achieved status (earned, e.g., doctor).
• Social institutions. Enduring patterns organising key needs — family & kinship, economy, polity, religion, education. They provide stability but can also reproduce inequality.
• Culture, norms, values. Culture = shared ways of living (material & non-material). Norms = rules of behaviour; values = shared ideas of the desirable; mores (strong moral norms) vs folkways (everyday customs).
• Founders & perspectives (intro): Comte (coined 'sociology'), Durkheim (social facts, solidarity), Marx (class conflict), Weber (interpretive understanding, verstehen).
FACT/INTERPRETATION/OPINION. FACT: Durkheim studied suicide rates sociologically. INTERPRETATION: He argued suicide reflects social integration. OPINION: "Modern society is worse than traditional society" — a value claim.
DEFINITIONS (10). sociology, sociological imagination, society, social structure, socialisation, status, role, ascribed status, social institution, norm.
Q&A. Easy: Who coined 'sociology'? (Comte.) Primary agent of socialisation? (Family.) Medium: Difference between ascribed and achieved status? Application: Is unemployment a personal trouble or public issue? (Both — sociological imagination links them.) Reasoning: Why is socialisation essential for society? (Transmits culture; without it, norms wouldn't persist.)
MCQs (10, abridged). Coined 'sociology': Comte; caste is an  status: ascribed; expected behaviour of a position: role; lifelong learning of norms: socialisation; family is the  agent: primary; strong moral norms: mores; 'social facts' associated with: Durkheim; class conflict with: Marx; verstehen with: Weber; stable patterning of relations: social structure.
T/F (5). Sociology is just common sense. F. Status is a social position. T. Achieved status is fixed at birth. F. Family is a primary agent of socialisation. T. Norms are shared ideas of the desirable. F (that's values).
FILL BLANKS (5).  coined the term sociology (Comte). Behaviour expected of a status is a  (role).  links personal troubles to public issues (sociological imagination). Learning society's norms is  (socialisation). Caste is an  status (ascribed).
HOTS. Why can the same individual hold several statuses that conflict (role conflict)? (Multiple roles make competing demands.) How might an institution both stabilise and perpetuate inequality? (e.g., education transmits skills but can reproduce class advantage.)
MISCONCEPTIONS. "Sociology = social work" → it studies society scientifically. "Individuals act purely freely" → structures shape choices. "Status means prestige only" → it's any social position.
REVISION. Sociology & its imagination; society/structure; socialisation & agents; status/role, ascribed/achieved; institutions; culture/norms/values; founders.
ADAPTIVE — "sociological imagination". L1: "It's seeing how society shapes your personal life." L2: "It links private troubles (I'm jobless) to public issues (the economy shed jobs) — your biography meets history and society." L3: "Mills' concept: the capacity to situate individual experience within broader social-structural and historical contexts." L4: "It's like zooming out from your single dot to see the whole pattern of dots you're part of." L5: "Understanding that your friend's stress before board exams is partly a society-wide pressure, not just personal, is the sociological imagination at work."

===== PSYCHOLOGY =====

★ CHAPTER G11PSY-INTRO — Nature, Methods & Core Concepts of Psychology — FULLER ANCHOR
METADATA. Grade 11 · NCERT · Psychology · Prereq: none · Difficulty: Medium · Objectives: define psychology; its goals & subfields; scientific methods; understand a core process (learning) and key concepts (behaviour, cognition, the biological basis).
TEACHING.
• What is psychology? The scientific study of behaviour and mental processes (thinking, feeling, perceiving). Goals: describe, explain, predict and (help) control/change behaviour. It is a science because it uses systematic, empirical methods.
• Fields: cognitive, developmental, social, clinical, educational, organisational, health, and more.
• Methods: observation, experiment (manipulating an independent variable to see its effect on a dependent variable, with control), correlation (relationship, not causation), case study, survey, psychological testing. Ethics: informed consent, confidentiality, no harm.
• Perspectives (schools): psychodynamic (Freud — unconscious), behaviourist (Watson/Skinner — observable behaviour/learning), humanistic (Maslow/Rogers — growth), cognitive (mental processing), biological (brain/nervous system).
• Biological basis: the neuron, nervous system (central & peripheral), the brain's major parts; behaviour has a physical substrate.
• A core process — learning: a relatively permanent change in behaviour due to experience.
 • Classical conditioning (Pavlov): a neutral stimulus paired with a natural one comes to trigger a response (bell → salivation).
 • Operant conditioning (Skinner): behaviour is shaped by consequences — reinforcement (increases behaviour) and punishment (decreases it).
 • Observational learning (Bandura): learning by watching models.
FACT/INTERPRETATION/OPINION. FACT: Pavlov's dogs salivated to a bell after pairing. INTERPRETATION: This shows associative learning. OPINION: "Rewards are always better than punishment" — a value/empirical claim needing evidence.
DEFINITIONS (10). psychology, behaviour, cognition, independent variable, dependent variable, correlation, classical conditioning, operant conditioning, reinforcement, observational learning.
Q&A. Easy: Psychology studies behaviour and ? (Mental processes.) Who studied classical conditioning? (Pavlov.) Medium: Difference between reinforcement and punishment? Difficult: Why does correlation not imply causation? (A third variable or reverse direction may explain it.) Application: Giving a child a star for homework uses? (Positive reinforcement.) Reasoning: Why is control important in an experiment? (To isolate the effect of the independent variable.)
MCQs (10, abridged). Psychology is the science of behaviour and mental processes; Pavlov → classical conditioning; Skinner → operant conditioning; a reward that increases behaviour = reinforcement; watching a model = observational learning; variable manipulated = independent; relationship without causation = correlation; Freud → psychodynamic; Maslow → humanistic; the basic unit of the nervous system = neuron.
T/F (5). Psychology is not a science. F. Reinforcement decreases behaviour. F (increases). Correlation proves causation. F. Classical conditioning was studied by Pavlov. T. The neuron is a nervous-system cell. T.
FILL BLANKS (5). Psychology studies behaviour and  processes (mental).  conditioning pairs stimuli (Classical).  increases a behaviour (Reinforcement). The variable manipulated is the  variable (independent). Bandura studied  learning (observational).
HOTS. A study finds ice-cream sales and drowning both rise together — does ice-cream cause drowning? (No — a third variable, summer heat.) Why might punishment have side effects that reinforcement doesn't? (Fear/aggression; it doesn't teach the desired behaviour.)
MISCONCEPTIONS. "Psychology is just common sense/reading minds" → it's an empirical science. "Correlation = causation" → not necessarily. "Reinforcement = bribery always" → it's a systematic learning principle.
REVISION. Definition & goals; fields; methods & ethics; perspectives; biological basis; learning (classical/operant/observational).
ADAPTIVE — "reinforcement". L1: "A reinforcement is anything that makes a behaviour more likely to happen again." L2: "If a behaviour is followed by a reward (positive reinforcement) or by removing something unpleasant (negative reinforcement), it happens more often — like a star sticker making a child study more." L3: "Reinforcement is a consequence that increases the probability of the preceding operant response; positive adds a stimulus, negative removes an aversive one." L4: "Reinforcement is like watering a plant behaviour — the more you 'water' (reward) it, the more it grows." L5: "Apps use reinforcement — points, streaks, badges — to keep you coming back; it's operant conditioning in your pocket."
