CHAPTER G1M-1 — Pre-number Concepts ("Finding the Furry Cat!")

METADATA. Grade 1 · Board NCERT/CBSE · Subject Mathematics · Unit Number readiness · Prerequisites: KG counting to 10 · Difficulty: Easy · Objectives: use position words (in/out, above/below, near/far); compare (big/small, more/less, heavy/light); sort and classify objects; recognise "as many as" (one-to-one matching).

TEACHING MATERIAL.
Before numbers come ideas about quantity and position. A child must be able to say where a thing is and whether one group has more than another before formal counting means anything.
• Position/spatial words. Inside/outside (the cat is inside the basket), above/below, on/under, near/far, front/behind, left/right. These describe where things are.
• Comparison words. Big/small, tall/short, long/short, heavy/light, more/less/equal, fast/slow. Comparison always needs two things: "The elephant is bigger than the cat."
• Sorting/classifying. Grouping things that are alike — by colour, shape, size, or kind. Sorting trains the logic that underlies place value later.
• One-to-one matching ("as many as"). Draw a line from each cup to one saucer. If none is left over on either side, there are "as many cups as saucers" — equal. This is the seed of the equals sign.

Visual/diagram description. A basket with three kittens inside and two outside; arrows label inside/outside. Below, cups matched to saucers by connecting lines.

Common misconceptions. "The taller glass has more water" — not always; a wide short glass can hold more. Compare by matching or measuring, not by looking. "More things = takes more space" — five marbles take less space than one football.

DEFINITIONS (10). 1. Compare — to see how two things differ in size/number. 2. Sort — group alike things. 3. Classify — sort by a chosen rule. 4. More — greater amount. 5. Less — smaller amount. 6. Equal / as many as — same number. 7. Inside — within the boundary. 8. Outside — beyond the boundary. 9. Heavy — hard to lift; more weight. 10. One-to-one matching — pairing each item of one group with one of another.

Q&A. Easy: Is a bus big or small next to a cycle? (Big.) Where is a fish — inside or outside water? (Inside.) Medium: If 4 spoons match 4 bowls with none left, are they equal? (Yes.) Sort: apple, ball, banana — by "fruit." (Apple, banana.) Application: You have 3 red and 5 blue beads — which is more? (Blue.) Put the toy under the table — where is it? (Below the table.)

MCQs (10, abridged). 1. Opposite of big — (A)small (B)tall (C)more (D)up. 2. The cat sits  the box (it's covered): (A)inside (B)far (C)fast (D)equal. 3. Which is heavy? (A)feather (B)stone (C)paper (D)leaf. 4. 3 cups, 3 plates matched, none left → (A)more cups (B)more plates (C)equal (D)none. 5. Which holds more? (A)spoon (B)bucket (C)cup (D)glass. 6. Opposite of fast — (A)slow (B)big (C)near (D)more. 7. Sort out the odd one: dog, cat, car, cow → car (not an animal). 8. Fewer: 2 or 6? (A)2 (B)6. 9. Near is opposite of — (A)far (B)small (C)under. 10. A group of only round things should include — (A)ball (B)book (C)box (D)brick.

T/F (5). More means a smaller amount — F (greater). A stone is heavier than a feather — T. Inside and outside mean the same — F. Matching each cup to a saucer checks equality — T. A short wide glass can hold more than a tall thin one — T.

FILL BLANKS (5). Opposite of tall is  (short). If none is left over after matching, the groups are  (equal). A rock is  than a leaf (heavier). The cat is  the basket when covered (inside). 6 is  than 2 (more).

HOTS. Two glasses look different but hold the same water — how can you check? (Pour one into the other.) You matched forks to plates and 1 fork is left over — which is more? (Forks, by one.)

MISCONCEPTIONS corrected. Taller ≠ more (depends on width). More objects ≠ more space. Comparison needs two items, not one.

REVISION. Position words, comparison words (need two things), sorting by a rule, one-to-one matching → equal/more/less.

ADAPTIVE — "as many as / equal". L1: "Equal means the same number." L2: "Match each cup to a saucer; if none is left, there are as many cups as saucers — equal." L3: "Equality of two sets means a bijection exists — every element pairs with exactly one in the other set." L4: "Like musical chairs where every child gets a chair and no chair is empty — a perfect match." L5: "One roti per person at dinner: if plates and rotis run out together, they were equal."

CHAPTER G1M-2 — Shapes ("What is Long? What is Round?")
METADATA. Grade 1 · NCERT · Maths · Unit Geometry basics · Prereq G1M-1 · Easy · Objectives: identify 2-D shapes (circle, triangle, square, rectangle) and 3-D solids (ball/sphere, box/cuboid, can/cylinder); describe by rolls/slides, corners, sides; find shapes in surroundings.

TEACHING MATERIAL. Flat (2-D) shapes have length and width but no thickness — circle, triangle, square, rectangle. Solid (3-D) shapes take up space — a ball (sphere) rolls, a box (cuboid) slides and stacks, a can (cylinder) rolls on its side and stacks on its ends, a dice (cube) has 6 equal faces. We describe solids by whether they roll or slide, how many corners, faces, and edges. Circle: curved, no corner. Triangle: 3 sides, 3 corners. Square: 4 equal sides. Rectangle: 4 sides, opposite sides equal.

Table — solids.
 Solid  |  Rolls?  |  Slides/Stacks?  |  Real example 
------------------------------
 Sphere (ball)  |  Yes  |  No  |  football 
 Cube (dice)  |  No  |  Yes  |  dice, box 
 Cuboid (brick)  |  No  |  Yes  |  matchbox 
 Cylinder (can)  |  Yes (on side)  |  Stacks on ends  |  tin, glass 
 Cone  |  Rolls in circle  |  Stacks point-up  |  ice-cream cone 
Common misconceptions. A ball is not a "circle" — a circle is flat, a ball is a solid (sphere). A square is a special rectangle (all sides equal), but at Grade 1 we treat them as separate named shapes.

DEFINITIONS (10). flat shape, solid shape, circle, triangle, square, rectangle, corner (vertex), side, roll, slide.

Q&A. Easy: How many corners has a triangle? (3.) Does a ball roll? (Yes.) Medium: Name a shape with 4 equal sides (square). Which solid can both roll and stack? (Cylinder.) Application: Find a rectangle in the room (door/book). Which solid is a dice? (Cube.)

MCQs (10, abridged). Ball is a — sphere; dice has 6 faces; triangle has 3 corners; a can is a cylinder; circle has 0 corners; door ≈ rectangle; which rolls: ball; square has 4 equal sides; ice-cream cone shape: cone; stack biscuits like a — cylinder (tin).

T/F (5). A ball is a flat shape — F (solid). A cube has 6 faces — T. A circle has 3 corners — F (none). A cylinder can roll — T. A rectangle has 4 sides — T.

FILL BLANKS (5). A triangle has  sides (3). A  rolls (ball/sphere). A dice has  faces (6). A circle has  corners (0). A door is like a  (rectangle).

HOTS. Why can you stack cans but a ball rolls away? (Flat ends vs. curved surface.) Sort solids into "rolls" and "does not roll." (Rolls: ball, cylinder, cone; not: cube, cuboid.)

REVISION. 2-D flat vs 3-D solid; corners/sides/faces; roll vs slide; real-world shapes.

ADAPTIVE — "sphere vs circle". L1: "A circle is flat; a ball is round like an orange." L2: "A circle is drawn on paper (2-D); a sphere is a solid you can hold (3-D)." L3: "A circle is a 2-D locus of points equidistant from a centre; a sphere is its 3-D analogue." L4: "A circle is the shadow a ball casts on the ground — flat — while the ball itself is full and solid." L5: "A coin's face is a circle; the marble is a sphere."

CHAPTER G1M-3 — Numbers 1 to 9 ("Mango Treat")
METADATA. Grade 1 · NCERT · Maths · Prereq counting readiness · Easy · Objectives: count, read and write numerals 1–9; understand cardinality (last count = how many); order and compare; recognise zero as "none"; ordinal ideas (first–ninth).

TEACHING MATERIAL. Each number has a quantity, a number-word, and a numeral. Zero (0) means nothing/none — an empty plate has 0 mangoes. Cardinality: the last number you say when counting tells how many altogether. Ordinals: first, second, third… tell position in a line. We compare using more/less/equal and the words greater/smaller. Writing numerals: correct stroke order (e.g., 2 starts with a curve then a flat base).

Table. number-word ↔ numeral ↔ quantity for 0–9 (zero=•empty; one=•; two=••; … nine=•••••••••).

Common misconceptions. Zero is a number (means "none"), not "nothing to write." Counting the last word = total (cardinality), not just reciting. Numeral reversal (writing 3 backwards, or 6/9 confusion) — corrected by starting-point cues.

DEFINITIONS (10). numeral, number-word, zero, count, cardinal number, ordinal number, greater than, smaller than, order, digit.

Q&A. Easy: Write the numeral for seven (7). How many are in an empty box? (0.) Medium: Which is greater, 6 or 9? (9.) Who is 3rd in line if Asha is 1st? (the third child.) Application: Arrange 4, 1, 7 smallest first (1,4,7). Count 8 mangoes and say the total (8).

MCQs (10, abridged). Numeral for five: 5; "none" is written as 0; greater of 3 and 8: 8; after 6 comes 7; before 5 is 4; 2nd position word: second; smallest of 9,2,5: 2; how many dots ••• : 3; largest 1-digit number: 9; order 3,1,2 smallest-first: 1,2,3.

T/F (5). Zero means none — T. 9 is smaller than 6 — F. The last count word tells how many — T. After 8 comes 10 — F (9). "First" tells position — T.

FILL BLANKS (5). After 4 comes  (5).  means none (0/zero). The greatest 1-digit number is  (9). Before 7 is  (6). 3rd position is called  (third).

HOTS. If you eat all 5 mangoes, how many are left and what number is that? (0.) Riya is 2nd, Sam is just behind her — what position is Sam? (3rd.)

REVISION. 0–9 quantity/word/numeral; zero=none; cardinality; ordinals; compare & order.

ADAPTIVE — "zero". L1: "Zero means nothing is there." L2: "If you had 3 sweets and ate all 3, you have 0 — zero — left." L3: "Zero is the additive identity and the cardinality of the empty set." L4: "Zero is an empty basket — it's still a basket (a number), just with nothing in it." L5: "The scoreboard shows 0 before the match starts — no goals yet."

CHAPTER G1E-1 — Early Literacy Skills (anchor)
METADATA. Grade 1 · NCERT · English · Unit 1–2 skills · Prereq KG phonics · Easy · Objectives: revise letter sounds; blend/segment CVC words; read simple sentences; recognise naming words (nouns) and a/an; listen to a story and answer who/what/where; speak in short sentences; copy letters/words neatly.

TEACHING MATERIAL.
• Phonics & decoding. Sound out CVC and simple CVCC/CCVC words (cat, ship, frog). Segment to spell (dog → /d/ /o/ /g/).
• Sight words. the, a, is, in, it, and, to, I, you, we — read by memory.
• Naming words (nouns). Words for people, animals, places, things (boy, cow, park, ball). Names of people/places start with a capital letter.
• a / an. Use a before a consonant sound (a cat), an before a vowel sound (an apple).
• Sentence sense. A sentence starts with a capital and ends with a full stop; it tells a complete idea.
• Listening/speaking. Story-time builds vocabulary and sequence; picture-talk builds speaking.
• Handwriting. Correct formation on four lines; spacing between words.

Table — a/an. a book, a dog, a table  |  an apple, an egg, an umbrella.

Common misconceptions. "an" is used before vowel sounds, not just vowel letters (an hour). All words don't start with capitals — only sentence-starts and names.

DEFINITIONS (10). letter, sound, word, sentence, noun/naming word, vowel, consonant, capital letter, full stop, sight word.

Q&A. Easy: Read: "The cat is fat." Who is fat? (The cat.) Medium: a or an before "egg"? (an.) Name three animals (nouns). Application: Make a sentence with "dog." Point to a naming word in the room.

MCQs (10, abridged). a/an before apple: an; naming word: ball (not run); sentence ends with: full stop; capital needed for a person's name; blend d-o-g: dog; sight word: the; a  dog: a; vowel among b,e,t: e; start a sentence with a  letter: capital; "an" before: egg.

T/F (5). Names begin with a capital — T. Use "an" before "cat" — F (a cat). A sentence needs a full stop — T. "Ball" is a naming word — T. Sight words are sounded out letter by letter — F.

HOTS. Why "an hour" but "a house" when both start with h? (Sound: hour starts with a vowel sound, house with /h/.) Turn "cat" into a naming-word sentence. (e.g., "The cat ran.")

REVISION. decode CVC, sight words, nouns, a/an by sound, sentence = capital + full stop.

ADAPTIVE — "noun". L1: "A naming word names a person, animal, place or thing." L2: "boy, cat, park, ball are nouns — they name something." L3: "A noun is a word denoting an entity — concrete or abstract — that can head a noun phrase." L4: "Nouns are the name-tags of language — every person, place and thing wears one." CHAPTER G2M-1 — Place Value & Numbers to 100+ (anchor)
METADATA. Grade 2 · NCERT · Maths · Prereq G1 numbers to 99 · Medium · Objectives: read/write/order numbers to 100 (and beyond to 999 informally); expand into tens & ones; compare using < > =; skip-count by 2s/5s/10s; identify even/odd.

TEACHING MATERIAL.
• Place value. In a two-digit number the left digit is tens, the right is ones: 47 = 40 + 7 = 4 tens + 7 ones. Expanded form: 63 = 60 + 3.
• Comparing/ordering. Compare tens first, then ones. Symbols: > greater, < smaller, = equal (the open mouth "eats" the bigger number).
• Skip counting. By 2s (2,4,6…), 5s (5,10,15…), 10s (10,20,30…) — foundation for multiplication.
• Even/odd. Even numbers pair up with none left (2,4,6,8,0 at the end); odd have one left over (1,3,5,7,9 at the end).

Table — place value of 58.  |  Tens  |  Ones  |  =  |  5  |  8  |  → 50 + 8.

Common misconceptions. 45 vs 54 — same digits, different value (place matters). "Bigger digit means bigger number" — compare place by place from the left. The symbol < / > direction confuses many; teach "open side faces the bigger number."

DEFINITIONS (10). place value, tens, ones, expanded form, compare, greater than (>), smaller than (<), skip count, even, odd.

Q&A. Easy: What is the tens digit in 72? (7.) Even or odd: 8? (Even.) Medium: Expand 56 (50+6). Which is greater, 34 or 43? (43.) Application: Skip-count by 5 to 25 (5,10,15,20,25). Put >,<,= : 27  72 (<).

MCQs (10, abridged). Tens digit of 39: 3; 45 expanded: 40+5; 61  16: >; even number: 10; skip by 2 after 6: 8; smallest of 23,32,22: 22; 7 tens 0 ones = 70; odd number: 15; number after 99: 100; compare 50  50: =.

CHAPTER G2M-2 — Addition & Subtraction with Regrouping (anchor, condensed)
METADATA. Grade 2 · NCERT · Maths · Prereq place value · Medium · Objectives: add/subtract 2-digit numbers with and without regrouping; use place-value columns; solve one-step word problems; check by reversing.

TEACHING MATERIAL. Add ones first, then tens. Carrying (regrouping in addition): if the ones add to 10 or more, carry one ten to the tens column (e.g., 27 + 15: 7+5=12 → write 2, carry 1; 1+2+1=4 → 42). Borrowing (regrouping in subtraction): if the top ones digit is too small, borrow one ten (make it 10 ones) (e.g., 42 − 15: 2−5 can't, borrow → 12−5=7; 3−1=2 → 27). Addition and subtraction are inverse — check 42−15=27 by 27+15=42.

Worked example. 38 + 46: ones 8+6=14 → write 4 carry 1; tens 3+4+1=8 → 84. Check: 84−46=38. ✓

Common misconceptions. Forgetting to carry; subtracting the smaller ones digit from the larger regardless of position (writing 42−15 ones as 5−2=3). Teach column alignment.

DEFINITIONS (10). sum, difference, regroup, carry, borrow, column, place-value alignment, inverse operations, one-step problem, check.

Q&A/MCQ/etc. (condensed). 27+15=42; 53−28=25; carry needed in 46+37? Yes; borrow in 71−36? Yes; check 60−24 by 36+24=60; word: "23 apples + 19 = ?" 42.

HOTS. Find the missing digit: 3 + 15 = 52 (→ 37). Why must ones line up under ones? (Same place values add together.)

ADAPTIVE — "carrying". L1: "When ones make ten or more, one ten moves to the tens column." L2: "7+5=12: keep 2 ones, carry the 1 ten next door." L3: "Regrouping conserves value across positional columns during addition overflow." L4: "Like a box that holds only 9 marbles — the 10th forces you to start a new box (a ten)." L5: "Buying items: 7 loose ₹1 coins + 5 = 12 → exchange 10 coins for one ₹10 note (a ten)."

===== GRADE 1 (Maharashtra) =====

SUBJECT: MARATHI (Balbharati) — Anchor
METADATA. Grade 1 · Maharashtra · Marathi (first language) · Prereq: spoken Marathi · Easy · Objectives: recognise and write मुळाक्षरे (vowels स्वर + consonants व्यंजन); read simple बारखडी (consonant+vowel matra combinations); read/write 2–3 letter words; picture-word reading; recite रांगोळी/rhymes; speak in short sentences.

TEACHING MATERIAL (original).
• स्वर (vowels): अ आ इ ई उ ऊ ए ऐ ओ औ अं अः. These are spoken with an open voice and can stand alone.
• व्यंजन (consonants): क ख ग घ … ह ळ क्ष ज्ञ. A consonant carries an inherent 'अ' sound (क = "ka").
• बारखडी (matra chart): each consonant combines with each vowel sign to form a syllable: क का कि की कु कू के कै को कौ कं कः. Reading Marathi = reading these syllables fluently.
• Words: join syllables into words — कमळ (lotus), मका (corn), घर (house). Read left to right.
• Reading/writing: correct stroke order; the top horizontal line (शिरोरेषा) joins letters.
• Listening/speaking: rhymes and stories build vocabulary and pronunciation.

Common misconceptions. A consonant is not silent — it already contains 'अ'. Matras (ा ि ी ु ू …) change the vowel sound, not the consonant. Reading is syllable-by-syllable, not letter-by-letter.

DEFINITIONS (10). मुळाक्षरे (alphabet), स्वर (vowel), व्यंजन (consonant), बारखडी (syllable chart), मात्रा/काना-मात्रा (vowel sign), अक्षर (syllable/letter), शब्द (word), वाक्य (sentence), शिरोरेषा (top line), उच्चार (pronunciation).
