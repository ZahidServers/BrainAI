===== PHYSICS =====

★ FLAGSHIP G12P-ES — Electric Charges and Fields (Electrostatics) — FULL APPARATUS
1. METADATA
• Grade 12 · NCERT · Science · Physics · Unit: Electrostatics · Chapter: Electric Charges and Fields
• Prerequisites: vectors, Coulomb-scale forces, area/flux idea, integration basics.
• Difficulty: Medium–Hard (opens the electrostatics unit; heavy in boards + JEE/NEET).
• Objectives: properties of charge; Coulomb's law (vector form); superposition; electric field & field lines; electric dipole & torque; continuous charge distributions; electric flux & Gauss's law with applications.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL
(a) Charge & its properties. Charge is a fundamental property of matter (unit coulomb, C). Two kinds: positive, negative; like repel, unlike attract. Key properties: quantisation (q = ne, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C), conservation (total charge is constant), and additivity (charges add algebraically).
(b) Coulomb's law. The force between two point charges: F = k·q₁q₂/r², k = 1/4πε₀ ≈ 9×10⁹ N·m²/C². Directed along the line joining them (repulsive for like, attractive for unlike). Vector form: F₁₂ = (1/4πε₀)(q₁q₂/r²) r̂. Weaker in a medium: divide by relative permittivity εᵣ.
(c) Superposition principle. The net force on a charge is the vector sum of forces due to all other charges, each computed independently.
(d) Electric field E. Field is force per unit positive test charge: E = F/q₀ (N/C or V/m), a vector. For a point charge: E = (1/4πε₀)(q/r²), pointing away from + and toward −. Fields obey superposition.
(e) Field lines. Imaginary lines showing field direction (tangent = E direction); denser lines = stronger field; start on +, end on −; never cross.
(f) Electric dipole. Two equal and opposite charges ±q separated by 2a. Dipole moment p = q·(2a), directed −→+. Field on axial line ≈ 2kp/r³; on equatorial line ≈ kp/r³ (opposite direction). In a uniform field, a dipole feels torque τ = pE sinθ (τ = p×E), zero net force; potential energy U = −pE cosθ.
(g) Continuous charge distributions. Linear (λ), surface (σ), volume (ρ) charge densities; field found by integrating dE over the distribution.
(h) Electric flux & Gauss's law. Flux Φ = E·A (Φ = ∮E·dA). Gauss's law: the net flux through a closed surface = enclosed charge / ε₀, Φ = qenc/ε₀. Powerful for symmetric charges:
 • Infinite line charge: E = λ/(2πε₀r).
 • Infinite charged sheet: E = σ/(2ε₀) (independent of distance).
 • Uniformly charged spherical shell: E = kq/r² outside (as if all charge at centre); E = 0 inside.
3. WORKED EXAMPLES
1. Two charges +2 μC and +3 μC are 0.3 m apart. F = 9×10⁹·(2×10⁻⁶·3×10⁻⁶)/(0.3²) = 9×10⁹·6×10⁻¹²/0.09 = 0.6 N (repulsive).
2. Field at 0.1 m from +5 μC: E = 9×10⁹·5×10⁻⁶/0.01 = 4.5×10⁶ N/C (away from charge).
3. Dipole p = 2×10⁻⁹ C·m in field 10⁵ N/C at 30°: τ = pE sinθ = 2×10⁻⁹·10⁵·0.5 = 1×10⁻⁴ N·m.
4. Flux through a closed surface enclosing +8.85 nC: Φ = q/ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻⁹/8.85×10⁻¹² = 1000 N·m²/C.

G. LONG-ANSWER (model)
Q: Using Gauss's law, derive the electric field due to a uniformly charged infinite plane sheet, and comment on its distance dependence.
Model: Choose a Gaussian "pillbox" cylinder piercing the sheet, faces of area A parallel to the sheet. By symmetry E is perpendicular to the sheet and equal on both faces; the curved side contributes no flux. Total flux = 2EA. Enclosed charge = σA. By Gauss: 2EA = σA/ε₀ → E = σ/2ε₀. It is independent of distance from the sheet (uniform field), directed away from a positively charged sheet.

H. HOTS
• Three equal charges at a triangle's vertices — is the net force on any one zero? (No, unless a special configuration; compute vector sum.)
• Why does a charged comb attract tiny paper bits (neutral)? (Induced polarisation creates a net attraction toward stronger field.)
• If flux through a closed surface is zero, must the enclosed charge be zero? (Yes, by Gauss's law — though field on the surface need not be zero.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS
• "Field lines are real, physical lines" → they are a representation.
• "Flux depends on the surface shape/size" → only on enclosed charge (Gauss).
• "Inside a charged shell the field is strong" → it is zero.
• "Coulomb force falls as 1/r" → it falls as 1/r².

===== CHEMISTRY =====

★ FLAGSHIP G12C-ELE — Electrochemistry — FULL APPARATUS
1. METADATA
• Grade 12 · NCERT · Science · Chemistry · Unit: Physical Chemistry · Chapter: Electrochemistry
• Prerequisites: redox reactions, oxidation numbers, mole concept, thermodynamics (ΔG).
• Difficulty: Medium–Hard (numericals-rich; JEE/NEET favourite).
• Objectives: distinguish galvanic vs electrolytic cells; standard electrode potentials & the electrochemical series; cell EMF and its relation to ΔG; the Nernst equation; conductance and molar conductivity (Kohlrausch); electrolysis and Faraday's laws; batteries, fuel cells, corrosion.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL
(a) Redox recap. Oxidation = loss of electrons; reduction = gain. Electrochemistry studies the interconversion of chemical and electrical energy via redox reactions occurring at electrodes.
(b) Galvanic (voltaic) cell. Spontaneous redox produces electricity. Two half-cells joined by a salt bridge; anode (oxidation, − terminal), cathode (reduction, + terminal). Example: Daniell cell, Zn  |  Zn²⁺  |    |  Cu²⁺  |  Cu. Electrons flow anode→cathode through the wire; current the opposite way.
(c) Electrode potential & the series. Each electrode has a tendency to be reduced, measured as standard electrode potential E° (vs the standard hydrogen electrode, SHE = 0 V). The electrochemical series ranks E°; higher E° = stronger oxidising agent (more easily reduced). Cell EMF: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. Positive E°cell ⇒ spontaneous.
(d) EMF–thermodynamics link. ΔG = −nFEcell (n = electrons transferred, F = 96500 C/mol). At standard state ΔG° = −nFE°cell; also ΔG° = −RT ln K, giving E°cell = (RT/nF) ln K.
(e) Nernst equation. Concentration dependence of potential:
 Ecell = E°cell − (RT/nF) ln Q = E°cell − (0.0591/n) log Q at 298 K.
 (Q = reaction quotient.) Explains how EMF changes as reactants deplete; at equilibrium Ecell = 0, Q = K.
(f) Conductance. Conductivity κ (S/cm) and molar conductivity Λm = κ×1000/c (S cm²/mol). Λm rises on dilution (more ions freed). Kohlrausch's law: at infinite dilution, Λ°m = sum of ionic contributions (λ°₊ + λ°₋).
(g) Electrolysis & Faraday's laws. Passing current through an electrolyte drives non-spontaneous redox (electrolytic cell). Faraday's 1st law: mass deposited ∝ charge (m ∝ Q = It). 2nd law: for the same charge, masses ∝ equivalent masses. One mole of electrons = 1 F = 96500 C.
(h) Applications. Primary cells (dry cell), secondary (lead-acid, Li-ion), fuel cells (H₂–O₂); corrosion as electrochemical (rusting) and its prevention (galvanising, cathodic protection).
3. WORKED EXAMPLES
1. Daniell cell: E°(Cu²⁺/Cu)=+0.34, E°(Zn²⁺/Zn)=−0.76. E°cell = 0.34−(−0.76) = +1.10 V (spontaneous).
2. ΔG° for the above (n=2): ΔG° = −nFE° = −2×96500×1.10 = −212,300 J = −212.3 kJ.
3. Nernst: for the cell with [Zn²⁺]=0.1, [Cu²⁺]=1.0 at 298 K: E = 1.10 − (0.0591/2) log(0.1/1.0) = 1.10 − 0.0296×(−1) = 1.13 V.
4. Electrolysis: current 2 A for 965 s deposits how many mol e⁻? Q=It=1930 C → 1930/96500 = 0.02 mol e⁻.
5. Molar conductivity: κ=0.025 S/cm, c=0.5 mol/L → Λm = 0.025×1000/0.5 = 50 S cm²/mol.

Daniell cell: left beaker Zn rod in ZnSO₄ (anode, −), right Cu rod in CuSO₄ (cathode, +), a salt bridge (KCl) connecting them, electrons flowing through the external wire from Zn to Cu; a voltmeter reads 1.10 V.

G. LONG-ANSWER (model)
Q: Derive ΔG° = −nFE°cell and use it to compute ΔG° and K for a cell with E°cell = 0.59 V, n = 2, at 298 K.
Model: Electrical work done by the cell = charge × potential = (nF)(E°cell); this equals the maximum useful work = −ΔG°, so ΔG° = −nFE°cell. Compute: ΔG° = −2×96500×0.59 = −113,870 J ≈ −113.9 kJ. For K: ΔG° = −RT ln K → ln K = −ΔG°/RT = 113870/(8.314×298) ≈ 45.9 → K ≈ e⁴⁵·⁹ ≈ 9×10¹⁹ (very product-favoured).

• Why can't we measure a single electrode potential absolutely? (Only potential differences are measurable; SHE is the reference set to 0.)

• "Molar conductivity decreases on dilution" → it increases (more ions dissociate/move freely).

★ FLAGSHIP G12B-GEN — Principles of Inheritance and Variation (Genetics) — FULL APPARATUS
1. METADATA
• Grade 12 · NCERT · Science · Biology · Unit: Genetics & Evolution · Chapter: Principles of Inheritance and Variation
• Prerequisites: cell division (meiosis), chromosomes, gene concept.
• Difficulty: Medium–Hard (top-scoring NEET unit).
• Objectives: Mendel's experiments & laws; monohybrid & dihybrid crosses; dominance/recessiveness; test cross; incomplete dominance & codominance; multiple alleles (blood groups); linkage & recombination; chromosomal theory; sex determination; pedigree analysis; genetic disorders.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL
(a) Mendel & his method. Gregor Mendel used garden pea (Pisum sativum) with clear contrasting traits and controlled crosses, tracking traits across generations (P, F₁, F₂) quantitatively.
(b) Terms. Gene (unit of heredity), allele (variant of a gene), genotype (genetic makeup) vs phenotype (appearance), homozygous/heterozygous, dominant/recessive.
(c) Law of Dominance. In a heterozygote, one allele (dominant) masks the other (recessive); the F₁ shows the dominant trait.
(d) Law of Segregation (purity of gametes). The two alleles of a gene separate during gamete formation, each gamete getting one; they rejoin at fertilisation. A monohybrid F₂ ratio is 3:1 phenotypic (1:2:1 genotypic).
(e) Law of Independent Assortment. Alleles of different genes assort independently (for genes on different chromosomes). A dihybrid F₂ ratio is 9:3:3:1. Punnett squares predict these.
(f) Test cross. Crossing an organism of unknown genotype with a homozygous recessive reveals whether it is homozygous (all dominant offspring) or heterozygous (1:1).
(g) Deviations from Mendel.
 • Incomplete dominance: heterozygote is intermediate (e.g., pink snapdragon; F₂ 1:2:1 phenotype = genotype).
 • Codominance: both alleles fully expressed (e.g., AB blood group).
 • Multiple alleles: more than two alleles in a population (ABO: Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i).
 • Pleiotropy: one gene affects many traits.
(h) Chromosomal theory & linkage. Genes are on chromosomes (Sutton & Boveri). Genes on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together (linkage); crossing over produces recombination, more frequent for genes far apart (Morgan's Drosophila work).
(i) Sex determination. Humans: XX (female), XY (male); the sperm decides sex. Other systems: XX–XO, ZZ–ZW (birds).
(j) Genetic disorders.
 • Mendelian: haemophilia & colour blindness (X-linked recessive), sickle-cell anaemia (autosomal recessive, codominant at molecular level), thalassaemia, phenylketonuria.
 • Chromosomal: Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Klinefelter (XXY), Turner (XO).
(k) Pedigree analysis. Family trees trace trait inheritance to deduce dominant/recessive and autosomal/sex-linked patterns.
3. WORKED EXAMPLES
1. Monohybrid: Tall(TT) × short(tt) → F₁ all Tt (tall); F₁ self → F₂ 3 tall : 1 short (TT:Tt:tt = 1:2:1).
2. Test cross of a tall pea: if offspring are all tall → TT; if 1 tall : 1 short → Tt.
3. Dihybrid: RrYy × RrYy → 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green.
4. Blood group: Iᴬi × Iᴮi → children Iᴬᴮ (AB), Iᴬi (A), Iᴮi (B), ii (O) — all four groups possible.
5. Colour blindness: carrier mother (XᶜX) × normal father (XY) → sons ½ colour-blind (XᶜY), daughters ½ carriers.
4. VISUAL / DIAGRAM DESCRIPTION
A 4×4 Punnett square for RrYy × RrYy producing the 9:3:3:1 grid; a pedigree chart with squares (males), circles (females), shaded = affected, showing X-linked recessive transmission through carrier mothers to sons.

G. LONG-ANSWER (model)
Q: A woman with blood group A and a man with blood group B have four children with groups A, B, AB and O. Explain the genetics.
Model: Blood-group O child means each parent carries a recessive i allele. So the mother is Iᴬi (group A) and the father Iᴮi (group B). Cross Iᴬi × Iᴮi → Iᴬi:Iᴬᴮ... using a Punnett square: offspring genotypes Iᴬi (A), Iᴬᴮ (AB), Iᴮi (B), ii (O) in 1:1:1:1 ratio — exactly the four observed groups. This illustrates multiple alleles (Iᴬ, Iᴮ, i) and codominance of Iᴬ and Iᴮ.

H. HOTS

• Why did Mendel's independent assortment hold — what would break it? (Genes on different chromosomes; linkage would break it.)
• If a plant with unknown genotype for a dominant trait gives a 1:1 ratio in a test cross, what is its genotype? (Heterozygous.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS
• "Dominant means more common or stronger" → it only means it masks the recessive in heterozygotes.
• "Blood group O parents can have an AB child" → impossible (O = ii).
• "Traits blend" → generally particulate inheritance (except incomplete dominance appears blended but alleles stay discrete).
• "The mother determines the baby's sex" → the sperm (X or Y) does.

★ CHAPTER G12M-INT — Integrals — MEDIUM ANCHOR
METADATA. Grade 12 · NCERT · Maths · Unit: Calculus · Prereq: differentiation, functions · Difficulty: Hard · Objectives: understand integration as anti-derivative; standard integrals; methods (substitution, parts, partial fractions); definite integrals & the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus; area interpretation.
TEACHING. Integration is the inverse of differentiation: if d/dx F(x) = f(x), then ∫f(x)dx = F(x) + C (indefinite integral; C is the constant of integration). Standard integrals: ∫xⁿdx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)+C (n≠−1); ∫(1/x)dx = ln  |  x  |  +C; ∫eˣdx = eˣ+C; ∫sinx dx = −cosx+C; ∫cosx dx = sinx+C. Methods: substitution (change of variable), integration by parts (∫u v′ = uv − ∫u′v), and partial fractions (for rational functions). Definite integral ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = F(b) − F(a) (Fundamental Theorem), giving the signed area under the curve between a and b. Properties: ∫ₐᵇ = −∫ᵦᵃ; ∫ₐᵇ = ∫ₐᶜ + ∫꜀ᵇ.
Worked: ∫(3x²)dx = x³+C. ∫₀¹ x²dx = [x³/3]₀¹ = 1/3. ∫x·eˣ dx (by parts, u=x) = x·eˣ − ∫eˣ = eˣ(x−1)+C.
DEFINITIONS (8). integral, anti-derivative, indefinite integral, definite integral, constant of integration, substitution, integration by parts, Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
Q&A. Easy: ∫1 dx? (x+C.) ∫cosx dx? (sinx+C.) Medium: ∫x³dx? (x⁴/4+C.) ∫₀¹ 2x dx? (1.) Difficult: ∫x sinx dx by parts? (−x cosx + sinx + C.) Application: Area under y=x from 0 to 2? (∫₀² x dx = 2.) Reasoning: Why the +C? (Differentiation loses constants; the anti-derivative family differs by a constant.)
MCQs (8, abridged). ∫xⁿdx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1)+C; ∫(1/x)dx = ln  |  x  |  +C; ∫eˣdx = eˣ+C; ∫₀¹x²dx = 1/3; ∫sinx dx = −cosx+C; by-parts formula uv−∫u′v; definite integral gives signed area; FTC: ∫ₐᵇf = F(b)−F(a).
HOTS. Evaluate ∫₀^{π} sinx dx. (2.) Find ∫ (2x)/(x²+1) dx. (ln(x²+1)+C.) Why can a definite integral be negative? (Area below the x-axis counts as negative.)

ADAPTIVE — "integration". L1: "Integration adds up tiny pieces to find a total, like area under a curve." L4: "Differentiation slices a quantity into rates; integration glues the slices back into the whole — inverse operations." L5: "Integration finds distance from a speed graph, area of irregular land, or total charge from a current — summing continuous change."
