CHAPTER JR-LANG — Language & Early Literacy
1. CHAPTER METADATA
• Grade: Junior KG
• Board: Common pre-primary framework (aligns with NCERT/CBSE and Maharashtra Balbharati Foundational Stage)
• Stream: N/A (pre-primary)
• Subject: Language & Literacy (English; the same structure applies to Marathi/Hindi)
• Unit: Foundational oral and pre-reading skills
• Chapter: Sounds, letters, words and listening
• Prerequisites: Ability to hear and speak simple words; no reading required
• Difficulty: Foundational
• Learning objectives: By the end, the child can (a) recognise most capital letters A–Z by sight, (b) produce the beginning sound of common letters, (c) name 100+ everyday objects, (d) listen to and enjoy a short story, (e) hold a crayon and make controlled strokes.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL

Introduction. Language begins with listening and speaking, long before reading and writing. A Junior KG child already speaks in short sentences at home. Our job is to widen the child's spoken vocabulary, sharpen the ear for sounds, and gently introduce the shapes we call letters. Reading is not the goal yet — readiness is.

Section A — Listening. Listening is the first literacy skill. A child who listens well later reads well. We build it through: (i) sound games — "What do you hear?" (clap, bell, dog barking); (ii) following instructions — "Touch your nose, then clap"; (iii) story time — the adult reads slowly, points to pictures, and pauses so the child predicts what comes next.

Section B — Speaking (oral vocabulary). We name things the child can see and touch: body parts, family members, fruits, animals, colours, actions (run, jump, sit). Each new word is taught with a concrete object or picture, said aloud, and used in a tiny sentence: "This is an apple. The apple is red." Repetition across days is what fixes a word in memory.

Section C — Phonics (sounds first, letters second). Every letter has a name (say "bee" for B) and a sound (say /b/ as in ball). Junior KG focuses on the beginning sound of words. We do not blend words yet — that is Senior KG. Key idea: the sound /a/ starts apple, ant, arm.

Section D — Letter recognition. We begin with capital letters because they are visually simpler (straighter lines) than small letters. Children recognise letters as pictures before they can write them: A looks like a tent, O is a circle, S is a snake.

Section E — Pre-writing. Before letters come strokes: standing lines  |  , sleeping lines —, slanting lines / \, curves and circles. These are practised big first — in the air, in sand, on a large sheet — then smaller. Correct crayon grip (three fingers, "pinch and flip") is taught here.

Section F — Rhymes and stories. Rhymes teach rhythm, memory, and the rhyming pattern of language (cat–hat–bat). Stories teach sequence (beginning–middle–end) and new words in context.

Important facts.
• The English alphabet has 26 letters; each has a capital (uppercase) and a small (lowercase) form.
• A vowel is a letter that keeps the mouth open: A, E, I, O, U. All other letters are consonants.
• Reading readiness depends more on talk at home than on flashcards.

Common misconceptions.
• "Letters must be taught in A-to-Z order." Not true — sounds children hear most (m, s, a, t, p) are often taught first because they combine into early words.
• "Knowing letter names means the child can read." Names ≠ sounds. Reading needs sounds.

A. DEFINITIONS (10)
1. Letter — a written symbol that stands for a sound.
2. Alphabet — the full set of 26 English letters.
3. Capital letter — the big form of a letter (A, B, C).
4. Small letter — the little form (a, b, c).
5. Sound (phoneme) — the noise a letter makes, like /m/.
6. Vowel — the letters A, E, I, O, U.
7. Consonant — any letter that is not a vowel.
8. Rhyme — words that end with the same sound (cat, hat).
9. Stroke — a single pencil line (standing, sleeping, slanting, curve).
10. Story — a telling of events with a beginning, middle and end.

B. QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
Easy (5):
1. Q: What sound does B make? A: /b/ as in ball.
2. Q: Name a fruit that starts with A. A: Apple.
3. Q: How many letters are in the alphabet? A: 26.
4. Q: Is O a vowel? A: Yes.
5. Q: What is the small form of A? A: a.

Medium (5):
6. Q: Name two words that rhyme with cat. A: hat, bat (also mat, rat).
7. Q: Which letters are vowels? A: A, E, I, O, U.
8. Q: What comes at the beginning of a story? A: The beginning part that tells who and where.
9. Q: What sound starts the word sun? A: /s/.
10. Q: Give one standing-line letter and one curved letter. A: Standing — L or T; Curved — O or C.

Application (5):
11. Q: Your name starts with a big letter. If your name is Riya, what is the first letter? A: R.
12. Q: You see a dog. What sound does dog begin with? A: /d/.
13. Q: Sort these into "starts with M": mango, ball, moon. A: mango, moon.
14. Q: Point to something in the room that starts with /b/. A: (book, ball, bag — any correct object).
15. Q: Which word does NOT rhyme: pin, tin, dog? A: dog.

C. MULTIPLE CHOICE QUESTIONS (10 — age-appropriate set)
1. Which is a vowel? (A) B (B) E (C) M (D) T — E is one of A,E,I,O,U.
2. Ball begins with — (A) B (B) D (C) P (D) M — the /b/ sound.
3. Which rhymes with hat? (A) dog (B) cat (C) sun (D) pen — both end in -at.
4. How many vowels are there? (A) 3 (B) 5 (C) 10 (D) 26 — A,E,I,O,U.
5. The big form of c is — (A) C (B) o (C) e (D) s — capital C.
6. Sun starts with — (A) S (B) N (C) U (D) T.
7. Which is a curved-line letter? (A) L (B) T (C) O (D) I — O is a full curve.
8. Apple starts with — (A) A (B) P (C) L (D) E.
9. Which is NOT a letter? (A) M (B) 7 (C) Q (D) Z — 7 is a number.
10. A story usually has how many main parts? (A) 1 (B) 3 (C) 10 (D) 0 — beginning, middle, end.

D. TRUE / FALSE (5)
1. There are 26 letters in the alphabet. True.
2. B is a vowel. False — vowels are A, E, I, O, U; B is a consonant.
3. Cat and hat rhyme. True.
4. Small letters are bigger than capital letters. False — capital letters are the bigger form.
5. We should learn to write before we learn to listen. False — listening comes first.

4. Words that end with the same sound are said to . → rhyme
5. The big form of a letter is called a  letter. → capital

F. SHORT-ANSWER (exam-style, oral)
• Say the sounds of M, S, A.
• Clap the number of words: "The cat sat." (3 claps.)
• Name three animals and their first letters.

G. LONG-ANSWER (with model answer)
Q: Tell a short story about a hungry cat, in three parts.
Model: Beginning — A little cat woke up hungry. Middle — It looked for milk and found a bowl. End — It drank the milk and felt happy and slept. (Teacher checks for sequence, not grammar.)

H. HOTS
• If pin and tin rhyme, make one more word that rhymes. (win, bin, fin.)
• Your friend says "D" makes the /t/ sound. Is that right? Show the correct sound. (No; D says /d/.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS (corrected)
• "Reading the alphabet song = reading." → It builds order memory, not decoding.
• "Every word starts with a capital letter." → Only names and sentence-starts do.

J. REVISION
Key points: 26 letters; 5 vowels; each letter has a name and a sound; listening → speaking → pre-writing is the order; rhymes and stories build language.
Vocabulary: letter, sound, vowel, consonant, rhyme, stroke, story.
One-page note: Hear it, say it, see it, trace it.

ADAPTIVE EXPLANATIONS — Concept: "Vowel"
• L1 Beginner: "Vowels are five special letters: a, e, i, o, u."
• L2 Grade level: "When you say a, e, i, o, u your mouth stays open. Those five are called vowels."
• L3 Technical: "Vowels are speech sounds made without blocking air in the mouth; in English they are represented by a, e, i, o, u (and sometimes y)."
• L4 Analogy: "Vowels are like the doors that stay open — air walks straight through. Consonants are doors that partly close."
• L5 Real-world: "Almost every word you say has a vowel in it — say 'mummy', 'papa', 'apple' and feel your mouth open on the vowel."

CHAPTER JR-MATH — Early Mathematics
1. CHAPTER METADATA
• Grade: Junior KG · Subject: Early Mathematics · Unit: Number sense, shapes, patterns
• Prerequisites: None beyond speaking
• Difficulty: Foundational
• Learning objectives: Count objects 1–10 with one-to-one correspondence; recognise numerals 1–10; name basic shapes; copy simple patterns; sort and compare (big/small, more/less).
2. TEACHING MATERIAL

Introduction. Maths for a three-year-old is not sums on paper. It is touching, moving and comparing real things. Number sense grows from counting objects you can hold.

Section A — Counting (1–10). The most important early skill is one-to-one correspondence: touch one object, say one number. Many children can recite "1,2,3…" like a song but skip or double-count objects. We fix this by counting slowly with fingers and blocks, moving each object as it is counted.

Section B — Numeral recognition. The word "three," the quantity ●●●, and the symbol "3" are three different things a child must connect. We show all three together: three claps, three dots, the numeral 3.

Section C — Shapes. Circle (round, no corners), square (4 equal sides, 4 corners), triangle (3 sides, 3 corners), rectangle (4 sides, longer than a square). Children find shapes in real objects — a plate is a circle, a window is a square.

Section D — Patterns. A pattern is something that repeats: red–blue–red–blue. Children copy, then extend patterns. Patterns build the logical thinking needed later for maths.

Section E — Sorting & comparing. Sorting = putting like with like (all red buttons together). Comparing = big/small, tall/short, more/less, heavy/light. "More" and "less" are compared by lining objects up one-to-one.

Section F — Measurement, time, money (awareness only). Long/short, day/night, and recognising coins — no calculation yet.

------------------------------
 Circle  |  0 (curved)  |  0  |  ball, plate 
 Triangle  |  3  |  3  |  slice of pizza 
 Square  |  4 equal  |  4  |  biscuit, tile

Visual/diagram description. A "counting caterpillar": ten circles in a row, numbered 1–10, each with that many dots inside, so the child sees quantity grow.

Common misconceptions.
• "Bigger number word = comes later" confusion: a child may think a longer object is "more" even when counted objects are fewer. We correct by counting, not eyeballing.
• Skipping numbers while counting objects (counting the song, not the things).

A. DEFINITIONS (10)
1. Number — tells how many.
2. Numeral — the written symbol (1, 2, 3).
3. Count — to say how many, one at a time.
4. Shape — the form of a thing (circle, square…).
5. Circle — a round shape with no corners.
6. Square — a shape with 4 equal sides and 4 corners.
7. Triangle — a shape with 3 sides and 3 corners.
8. Pattern — something that repeats in order.
9. Sort — to put similar things together.
10. Compare — to see which is more, less, bigger or smaller.

B. QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
Easy: Count to 5. (1,2,3,4,5.) · How many sides does a triangle have? (3.) · Show 2 fingers. · Which is bigger, an elephant or an ant? (Elephant.) · What shape is a ball? (Circle.)
Medium: Continue the pattern: red, blue, red,  (blue). · Which has more: ●● or ●●●●? (●●●●.) · Name a square thing in the room. · Point to the number 4. · Sort into big and small: elephant, ant, cat. (Big: elephant; small: ant; cat: middle.)
Application: Put 3 blocks in the box. · There are 2 apples and 1 more comes — how many? (3.) · Line up 5 pencils and count them touching each one. · Find something round. · Which is tall: a tree or grass? (Tree.)

2. Which is round? (A) ball (B) box (C) book (D) door.
3. Count: ●●● = (A) 2 (B) 3 (C) 4 (D) 5.
4. Next in pattern red, blue, red,  : (A) blue (B) green (C) red (D) yellow.
5. A triangle has  sides: (A) 2 (B) 3 (C) 4 (D) 5.
6. Which is more: (A) 1 (B) 2 (C) 5 (D) 3.
7. Which shape is a door most like? (A) circle (B) triangle (C) rectangle (D) star.
8. The numeral after 2 is (A) 1 (B) 3 (C) 4 (D) 0.
9. Which is smallest? (A) elephant (B) dog (C) ant (D) cow.
10. How many fingers on one hand? (A) 3 (B) 4 (C) 5 (D) 10.

D. TRUE / FALSE (5)
1. A circle has 4 corners. False — a circle has no corners.
2. 3 comes after 2. True.
3. An ant is bigger than an elephant. False.
4. A pattern repeats. True.
5. Counting means touching each object once and saying one number. True.

E. FILL IN THE BLANKS (5)

F. SHORT-ANSWER (oral): Count out 6 blocks. Name three shapes. Make a red-yellow pattern with beads.

G. LONG-ANSWER (model): "Show me you can count to 10 with the toy cars." Model: child touches each of ten cars once, saying 1–10 without skipping — that is correct one-to-one counting.

H. HOTS: You have 2 sweets and your friend has 3. Who has more, and how many more? (Friend; 1 more.) · Can a shape be both a rectangle and have 4 corners? (Yes — a rectangle has 4 corners.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS: "The longer line always has more" → count them. "5 is always big" → 5 is small compared to 10.

ADAPTIVE — Concept: "More than"
• L1: "More means a bigger amount."

• L3: "'More than' is a comparison of two quantities where one set has a greater count."
• L4: "Like two lines of children: the longer line, when everyone is paired up, has 'more' — the extra kids with no partner show 'how many more.'"
• L5: "At the shop, ₹10 buys more than ₹5 — more money, more toffees."

CHAPTER JR-EVS — Environmental Awareness ("Myself and My World")
1. CHAPTER METADATA
• Grade: Junior KG · Subject: Environmental Awareness / General Knowledge · Unit: Self, family, and surroundings
• Prerequisites: None · Difficulty: Foundational
• Learning objectives: Name body parts and five senses; identify family members and their roles; name common animals, plants, foods; talk about water, weather, transport; know simple safety and hygiene habits.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL

Section A — Myself. Body parts (head, hands, legs, eyes, ears, nose, mouth). The five senses: see (eyes), hear (ears), smell (nose), taste (tongue), touch (skin). Feelings: happy, sad, angry, scared.

Section B — My family. Mother, father, brother, sister, grandparents. Families care for and help each other. Different families look different — all are okay.

Section C — My school. Teacher, friends, classroom things (board, bag, chair). School rules: share, take turns, listen.

Section D — Animals. Pets (dog, cat), farm animals (cow, hen), wild animals (lion, elephant). What they eat, the sounds they make, where they live.

Section E — Plants. Trees, flowers, grass. Plants give us fruits, vegetables, and clean air. We should not pluck leaves carelessly.

Section F — Food & water. Healthy food (fruits, vegetables, milk) vs. sometimes-food (sweets). Water: we drink it, wash with it, and must not waste it.

Section G — Weather. Sunny, rainy, cloudy, cold. What we wear and do in each.

Section H — Transport. Land (car, bus, cycle), water (boat), air (aeroplane).

Section I — Community helpers. Doctor, teacher, police, farmer, postman — people who help us.

Section J — Safety & hygiene. Wash hands before eating; brush teeth; don't touch fire, sharp things, or electric sockets; hold an adult's hand on the road.

------------------------------
 See  |  Eyes  |  look at things 
 Hear  |  Ears  |  listen to sounds 
 Smell  |  Nose  |  smell flowers, food 
 Taste  |  Tongue  |  taste sweet/sour

Common misconceptions. "Only mummy can cook / only papa can drive" — anyone can do any helpful job. "Wild animals make good pets" — no, they belong in the wild.

2. Family — the people we live with and who care for us.
3. Pet — a tame animal kept at home.
4. Wild animal — an animal that lives in forests, not homes.
5. Plant — a living thing that grows in soil and needs water and sunlight.
6. Weather — how the sky and air are (sunny, rainy).
7. Transport — ways we travel from place to place.
8. Community helper — a person whose job helps others.
9. Hygiene — keeping ourselves clean.

Easy: How many senses? (5.) · What do we see with? (Eyes.) · Name a pet. (Dog/cat.) · What do plants need? (Water and sunlight.) · Who teaches you? (Teacher.)
Medium: Which animal gives us milk? (Cow.) · What do we wear when it rains? (Raincoat, use umbrella.) · Name one thing that flies. (Aeroplane/bird.) · Why wash hands? (To stay clean/healthy.) · Who helps when we are sick? (Doctor.)

PART B — SENIOR KG (typical age 4–5 years)

Senior KG builds directly on Junior KG. The child moves from recognising to doing: from knowing letters to writing them and blending sounds into words; from counting to 10 to counting to 50–100 and simple addition/subtraction; from naming shapes to noticing their properties.


CHAPTER SR-LANG — Language & Literacy (Blending, Writing, Sentences)
1. CHAPTER METADATA
• Grade: Senior KG · Subject: Language & Literacy · Unit: Phonics blending, early reading & writing
• Prerequisites: JR-LANG (letter recognition, beginning sounds)
• Difficulty: Foundational-plus
• Learning objectives: Write capital and small letters; blend CVC words (c-a-t → cat); read simple 3-letter words; form a short spoken/written sentence; recognise sight words (the, a, is, I).
2. TEACHING MATERIAL

Section A — Small letters & writing. Now the child learns the small (lowercase) letters and practises writing on four-lined paper (top line, middle lines, base line). Letters have a starting point and a direction; we teach these consistently (e.g., 'a' starts at the middle, curls left).

Section B — Blending (the big new skill). Blending means joining sounds to make a word. CVC = consonant–vowel–consonant. Say the sounds separately, then faster: /c/ /a/ /t/ → cat. This is the doorway to reading.

Section C — Digraphs & word families. Word families share an ending: -at (cat, bat, hat), -an (man, fan, can), -ig (pig, dig, big). Learning a family lets a child read many words at once.

Section D — Sight words. Some common words don't sound out neatly (the, was, is). We learn these by sight through repetition.

Section E — Sentence formation. A sentence tells a complete idea, starts with a capital letter, and ends with a full stop. "The cat is big." Children build sentences orally first, then copy short ones.

Section F — Reading & comprehension. The child reads a two-line story and answers "who? what?" — the start of comprehension.

Table — CVC word families.
 Family  |  Words 
------------------------------
 -at  |  cat, bat, hat, mat, rat 
 -an  |  man, can, fan, pan, van 
 -ig  |  pig, big, dig, fig, wig 
 -ot  |  pot, hot, dot, cot, not 
Visual/diagram description. Three boxes side by side: [c][a][t] with a sliding arrow underneath that "pushes" the sounds together into the word "cat."

Common misconceptions. "Sounding out means saying letter names" — no, use sounds (/c/ not "see"). "Every word can be sounded out" — sight words like 'the' must be memorised.

A. DEFINITIONS (10)
1. Blend — to join sounds to read a word.
2. CVC word — a word with consonant–vowel–consonant (cat).
3. Word family — words sharing the same ending (-at).
4. Sight word — a common word learned by memory (the, is).
5. Sentence — a group of words giving a complete idea.
6. Full stop — the dot ending a sentence.
7. Capital letter — the big letter that starts a sentence or name.
8. Small letter — the lowercase form.
9. Reading — turning written words into their sounds and meaning.
10. Comprehension — understanding what is read.

B. QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
Easy: Blend /d/ /o/ /g/. (dog.) · What ends the -at family word "ht"? (a → hat.) · Write small 'a'. · Is "the" a sight word? (Yes.) · What ends a sentence? (Full stop.)

Application: Build a sentence with "cat." (e.g., "The cat is fat.") · Read "I am happy." How do you feel? (Happy.) · Find a word in the room and blend it.

9. -ig family: (A) pig (B) cat (C) man (D) pot.
10. "The dog ran." How many words? (A) 2 (B) 3 (C) 4 (D) 1.

D. TRUE / FALSE (5)
1. "cat" is a CVC word. True. 2. We sound out with letter names, not sounds. False — use sounds. 3. A sentence starts with a capital letter. True. 4. "the" can be easily sounded out. False — it's a sight word, learned by memory. 5. bat and hat are in the same word family. True.

• L4: "It's like a slide: each sound stands at the top, then slides down and bumps into the next — /c/…/a/…/t/ — until they land together as 'cat.'"
• L5: "When you read a shop sign or a food packet, you blend the letters to know what it says — that's reading in real life."

CHAPTER SR-MATH — Early Mathematics (Numbers to 100, Add/Subtract)
1. CHAPTER METADATA
• Grade: Senior KG · Subject: Early Mathematics · Unit: Bigger numbers, simple operations, measurement
• Prerequisites: JR-MATH (count 1–10, shapes, patterns)
• Difficulty: Foundational-plus
• Learning objectives: Count and write 1–50 (recognise to 100); understand "add" (put together) and "subtract" (take away) within 10; order numbers; compare using more/less/equal; tell simple time (day parts) and recognise coins.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL

Section A — Counting & writing 1–50 (to 100). Numbers continue in a pattern of tens: 10, 20, 30… The child counts in ones and begins counting in tens and (playfully) twos.

Section B — Before/after/between. "What comes after 7?" (8). "Before 5?" (4). "Between 3 and 5?" (4). This builds number order.

Section C — Addition (putting together). Add = combine two groups and count all. 2 apples + 3 apples = 5 apples. We use fingers and objects; the "+" and "=" signs are introduced.

Section D — Subtraction (taking away). Subtract = start with a group and remove some. 5 sweets − 2 sweets = 3 sweets. The "−" sign is introduced.

Section E — Comparing (more/less/equal). Using symbols later, but here in words: which group has more? are they equal?

Section F — Measurement. Long/short, tall/short, heavy/light, full/empty — compared by looking and by simple non-standard units (how many hand-spans long?).

Section G — Time & money. Parts of the day (morning, afternoon, night); days of the week (song); recognising ₹1, ₹2, ₹5, ₹10 coins.

Table — Number order.
 Before  |  Number  |  After 
------------------------------
 4  |  5  |  6 
 8  |  9  |  10 
 19  |  20  |  21 
Visual/diagram description. A "ten-frame": two rows of five boxes. Filling counters into it shows numbers up to 10 and makes addition visible (fill 3, add 2 more, count 5).

Common misconceptions. "Adding always makes a big number and it's hard" — adding is just counting on. "The number that sounds longer is bigger" — 100 has fewer syllables than "seventy-seven"; we compare by value, not word length. Writing 21 as "12" (reversing digits) is a common early slip — corrected by place-value talk (tens and ones).

A. DEFINITIONS (10)
1. Add / Addition — putting groups together to find the total.
2. Subtract / Subtraction — taking some away to find what's left.
3. Sum / Total — the answer in addition.
4. Plus (+) — the sign for adding.
5. Minus (−) — the sign for taking away.
6. Equal (=) — has the same value.
7. More — a greater amount.
8. Less — a smaller amount.
9. Before/After — the number just smaller/just bigger.
10. Between — the number in the middle of two.

B. QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
Easy: Count to 20. · What is 2 + 1? (3.) · What is 3 − 1? (2.) · What comes after 9? (10.) · Which is more: 4 or 7? (7.)
Medium: 4 + 3 = ? (7.) · 6 − 2 = ? (4.) · What is between 6 and 8? (7.) · Count in tens to 50. (10,20,30,40,50.) · Order: 5, 2, 8 smallest first. (2, 5, 8.)
Application: You have 3 toffees, mother gives 2 more — how many? (5.) · 5 birds sat, 2 flew away — how many left? (3.) · Which coin is bigger in value, ₹1 or ₹5? (₹5.) · Is it morning or night when you sleep? (Night.)

C. MCQs (10)
1. 2 + 3 = (A) 4 (B) 5 (C) 6 (D) 1.
2. 6 − 4 = (A) 2 (B) 3 (C) 10 (D) 1.
3. After 14 comes (A) 13 (B) 15 (C) 16 (D) 41.
4. Which is more? (A) 3 (B) 9 (C) 5 (D) 1.
5. Count in tens: 10, 20,  (A) 21 (B) 30 (C) 12 (D) 25.
6. The sign for adding is (A) − (B) + (C) = (D) ×.
7. 5 = 5 means they are (A) more (B) less (C) equal (D) added.
8. Between 7 and 9 is (A) 6 (B) 8 (C) 10 (D) 5.
9. We sleep at (A) morning (B) noon (C) night (D) evening play.
10. Which coin is worth most? (A) ₹1 (B) ₹2 (C) ₹5 (D) ₹10.

D. TRUE / FALSE (5)
1. 3 + 2 = 5. True. 2. Subtraction means putting together. False — it means taking away. 3. After 19 comes 20. True. 4. ₹5 is less than ₹1. False — ₹5 is more. 5. 7 is more than 4. True.

E. FILL IN THE BLANKS (5)
1. 4 + 4 = . → 8 2. 9 − 3 = . → 6 3. After 29 comes . → 30 4. The + sign means . → add/plus 5. Between 5 and 7 is . → 6

F. SHORT-ANSWER: Solve 3+2 and 5−1 with counters. Count in tens to 50. Name the parts of the day in order.

G. LONG-ANSWER (model): "Show me 4 + 3 using the ten-frame." Model: child places 4 counters, then 3 more, counts all seven, and says "4 + 3 = 7." Teacher checks counting-on and correct total.

H. HOTS: If 3 + 2 = 5, what is 2 + 3? (Also 5 — order doesn't change the sum.) · You have 6 balloons and 2 pop. If 1 more pops, how many are left? (3.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS: "Adding always needs new counting from 1" → you can count on from the bigger number. "21 and 12 are the same" → tens and ones differ; 21 = 2 tens 1 one.

J. REVISION: Count/write to 50 (recognise 100); count in tens; add = put together, subtract = take away; before/after/between; ₹ coins; parts of the day.
Formula-style facts: a + b = b + a (order doesn't change the sum for addition); taking all away leaves 0 (a − a = 0).

ADAPTIVE — Concept: "Addition"
• L1: "Adding means putting things together to see how many in all."
• L2: "If you have 2 apples and get 3 more, put them together and count: 5. That's 2 + 3 = 5."
• L3: "Addition combines two quantities (addends) to produce their total (sum); it is commutative, so a + b = b + a."
• L4: "Addition is like pouring two cups of water into one jug — the jug now holds both amounts together."
• L5: "At a party you had 4 balloons and a friend brings 3 — now you have 7 balloons to play with."

CHAPTER SR-EVS — Environmental Awareness (My Wider World)
1. CHAPTER METADATA
• Grade: Senior KG · Subject: Environmental Awareness · Unit: Living/non-living, seasons, our country, good habits
• Prerequisites: JR-EVS · Difficulty: Foundational-plus
• Learning objectives: Tell living from non-living; name the seasons and festivals; know the national symbols (flag, animal, bird); understand needs of living things (air, water, food); practise good manners and safety rules.
2. TEACHING MATERIAL

Section A — Living and non-living. Living things grow, need food/water/air, move on their own, and have babies (humans, animals, plants). Non-living things do not (stone, chair, toy). Careful: a car moves but is non-living — it needs a driver; plants don't run but are living.

Section B — Needs of living things. All living things need air, water, food, and space; plants also need sunlight. Take these away and living things cannot survive.

Section C — Seasons. In India: summer (hot), monsoon/rainy (wet), winter (cold). What we wear, eat and do changes with seasons.

Section D — Our country. India is our country. The national flag has three colours (saffron, white, green) with a blue wheel (Ashoka Chakra). National animal: tiger; national bird: peacock; national flower: lotus. We stand for the national anthem.

Section E — Festivals. People celebrate many festivals — Diwali, Eid, Christmas, Holi, Pongal — with lights, food, and family. We respect all festivals.

Section F — Good habits & manners. Say please, thank you, sorry; wait your turn; keep surroundings clean; don't waste food or water; be kind to animals.

Section G — Safety (extended). Know your name, parents' names, and home address; don't go with strangers; road safety (zebra crossing, traffic lights: red-stop, green-go).

Table — Living vs non-living.
 Feature  |  Living  |  Non-living 
------------------------------
 Grows  |  Yes  |  No 
 Needs food/water  |  Yes  |  No 
 Moves by itself  |  Yes  |  No (needs help) 
 Example  |  dog, tree  |  rock, chair 
Visual/diagram description. The Indian flag drawn with three horizontal bands and a 24-spoke blue wheel in the white band, labelled saffron (top), white (middle, with chakra), green (bottom).

Common misconceptions. "Anything that moves is alive" → cars and fans move but are non-living. "Plants are non-living because they don't move around" → plants are living; they grow and need water and sunlight. "The sun is alive because it gives light" → the sun is non-living.

A. DEFINITIONS (10)
1. Living thing — something that grows, needs food/water/air, and reproduces.
2. Non-living thing — something that does not grow or need food.
3. Season — a time of year with its own weather (summer, rainy, winter).
4. National flag — the special flag of our country.
5. Ashoka Chakra — the blue 24-spoke wheel on India's flag.
6. National animal — the animal that represents the country (tiger for India).
7. Festival — a special day people celebrate together.
8. Manners — polite ways of behaving.
9. Traffic light — the signal that tells vehicles to stop or go.
10. Address — where your home is located.

B. QUESTIONS & ANSWERS
Easy: Is a dog living or non-living? (Living.) · Name a season. (Summer/rainy/winter.) · What is India's national animal? (Tiger.) · Which light means stop? (Red.) · Say two magic words. (Please, thank you.)
Medium: Why is a chair non-living? (It doesn't grow, eat, or move by itself.) · What do we wear in winter? (Warm clothes/sweater.) · How many colours on our flag? (Three, plus the blue wheel.) · Name a festival of lights. (Diwali.) · What do plants need that animals don't specially need? (Sunlight to make food.)
Application:* Sort into living/non-living: cat, stone, tree, toy. (Living: cat, tree; non-living: stone, toy.) · The light turns red while crossing — what do you do? (Stop and wait.) · A stranger offers a toffee to go with them — what do you do? (Say no, tell a trusted adult.)

C. MCQs (10)
1. Which is living? (A) rock (B) tree (C) chair (D) ball.
2. India's national bird is the (A) crow (B) peacock (C) parrot (D) hen.
3. Red light means (A) go (B) stop (C) run (D) dance.
4. Which season is hot? (A) winter (B) rainy (C) summer (D) none.
5. All living things need (A) toys (B) air, water, food (C) phones (D) money.
6. A car moves but is (A) living (B) non-living (C) a plant (D) an animal.
7. Our flag's wheel is called the (A) sun (B) Ashoka Chakra (C) star (D) moon.
8. Diwali is a festival of (A) water (B) lights (C) snow (D) sand.
9. National animal of India: (A) lion (B) elephant (C) tiger (D) dog.
10. We should be  to animals: (A) unkind (B) kind (C) rough (D) scared.

D. TRUE / FALSE (5)
1. A tree is a living thing. True. 2. A car is living because it moves. False — it needs a driver; it is non-living. 3. India's national animal is the tiger. True. 4. Green light means stop. False — green means go; red means stop. 5. Living things need air, water and food. True.

1. A dog is a  thing. → living 2. Our national bird is the . → peacock 3.  light means stop. → Red 4. In winter we wear  clothes. → warm 5. Plants need sunlight, air, water and . → food/soil (space)

F. SHORT-ANSWER: Give two living and two non-living things. Name the three seasons in India. Say your full name and one parent's name.

G. LONG-ANSWER (model): "Tell me how to cross a road safely." Model: Stop at the edge; look right, left, right; wait for green/no cars; hold an adult's hand; walk (don't run) on the zebra crossing. Teacher checks the safety sequence.

H. HOTS: A toy car and a real kitten both move — which is living and how do you know? (The kitten: it grows, eats, and moves on its own; the car needs a person.) · Why can't a fish live out of water? (Living things need their proper needs met; a fish needs water to breathe.)

I. MISCONCEPTIONS: "Moving = alive" → cars/fans move but are non-living. "Plants are non-living" → plants grow and need water/sunlight; they are living. "Only red is a signal colour" → green and yellow are signals too.

J. REVISION: Living vs non-living (grow, eat, move by self); needs = air, water, food, (sunlight for plants); seasons = summer/rainy/winter; flag colours + Ashoka Chakra; national tiger/peacock/lotus; manners & road/stranger safety.

ADAPTIVE — Concept: "Living vs non-living"
• L1: "Living things grow and need food; non-living things don't."
• L2: "A plant grows, drinks water and makes seeds, so it's living. A stone never grows or eats, so it's non-living."
• L3: "Living organisms exhibit growth, metabolism, movement, response to stimuli, and reproduction; non-living matter shows none of these life processes."
• L4: "Think of a battery toy and a puppy. The toy moves only while the battery lasts and never grows. The puppy grows, gets hungry, and can have puppies — that's the difference between a machine and a living thing."
• L5: "In a garden, everything that will grow bigger and needs watering — grass, flowers, the caterpillar — is living; the fence, the pot, and the pebbles are non-living."
