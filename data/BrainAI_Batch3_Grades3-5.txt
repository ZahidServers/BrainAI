CHAPTER G3M-A — Multiplication ("Give and Take" / tables) — FULL APPARATUS
METADATA. Grade 3 · NCERT · Maths · Unit: Operations · Prereq: skip-counting, addition, arrays (G2) · Difficulty: Medium · Objectives: understand multiplication as repeated addition and as arrays; build and use tables (2–10); multiply a 2-digit number by a 1-digit number with and without regrouping; know properties (order/commutativity, ×1, ×0, ×10); solve word problems.

TEACHING MATERIAL.
Introduction. Multiplication is a fast way to add equal groups. Instead of 6+6+6+6, we say "4 groups of 6" = 4 × 6.
Meaning 1 — repeated addition. 4 × 6 means six added four times → 24. The first number = how many groups; the second = size of each group.
Meaning 2 — arrays. A rectangle of dots in 4 rows and 6 columns has 4 × 6 = 24 dots. Turning the array sideways (6 rows, 4 columns) still gives 24 — this shows order does not matter (commutative property): 4 × 6 = 6 × 4.
Meaning 3 — number line jumps. Start at 0 and jump 6 four times → lands on 24.
Key facts (properties). ×1 leaves a number unchanged (7×1=7); ×0 gives 0 (7×0=0); ×10 puts a 0 at the end (7×10=70); multiplying by 100 puts two zeros.
Tables. Each table is repeated addition of that number. Patterns help memory: the 5-table ends in 5 or 0; the 9-table digits add to 9 (9,18,27… → 9,9,9); the 10-table ends in 0.
2-digit × 1-digit (column method). Multiply ones, then tens, carrying when a product ≥10. Example 23 × 4: ones 3×4=12 → write 2 carry 1; tens 2×4=8, +1 = 9 → 92. With regrouping 27 × 6: 7×6=42 → 2 carry 4; 2×6=12, +4=16 → 162.

Worked examples.
1. 8 × 7 = 56 (memorise; check: 8×7 = 8×5 + 8×2 = 40+16 = 56).
2. 34 × 3: 4×3=12 (2 carry 1); 3×3=9, +1=10 → 102.
3. Word: "A box has 12 pencils. How many in 5 boxes?" → 12 × 5 = 60 pencils.

Visual/diagram description. A 4×6 grid of dots labelled "4 rows, 6 in each = 24"; beside it the same grid rotated to "6 rows, 4 in each = 24" to show commutativity.

DEFINITIONS (10). 1. Multiplication — repeated addition of equal groups. 2. Factor — a number being multiplied. 3. Product — the result. 4. Multiplicand/multiplier — the number multiplied / doing the multiplying. 5. Array — rows-and-columns arrangement. 6. Times table — list of a number's multiples. 7. Multiple — a product of a number and a whole number. 8. Commutative property — order doesn't change the product. 9. Regrouping/carrying — moving a ten during multiplication. 10. Identity (×1) / zero (×0) properties.

Q&A. Easy: What is 6×3? (18.) 9×1? (9.) 5×0? (0.) Medium: Write 7+7+7+7 as a multiplication (4×7=28). Is 3×8 the same as 8×3? (Yes, 24.) Difficult: 46×5? (230.) 38×7? (266.) Application: 6 rows of 8 chairs — how many chairs? (48.) A pencil costs ₹4; cost of 15? (₹60.) Reasoning: Why does 7×10 end in 0? (Multiplying by ten shifts every place up, adding a zero.)

MCQs (12, abridged). 4×6=24; 9×0=0; 8×1=8; 5×7=35; 12×3=36; 25×4=100; 7×10=70; commutative: 3×9=9×3; 6+6+6=3×6; 9-table digit sum of 27=9; array 5×4 dots=20; 23×4=92.

T/F (10). 4×6=6×4 T. 8×0=8 F (=0). Multiplying by 1 changes the number F. 7×10=70 T. 3×5 means 5 groups of 3 or 3 groups of 5 (same product) T. 12×5=50 F (=60). Every multiple of 5 ends in 5 or 0 T. An array shows multiplication T. 9×9=81 T. Order matters in multiplication F.

FILL BLANKS (10). 6×=42 (7). 8×5= (40). ×0=0 (any). 10×9= (90). 4×=24 (6). 7×7= (49). 3×=27 (9). 12×4= (48). ×1=15 (15). 5×6=6× (5).

SHORT-ANSWER. State the commutative property with an example. Compute 27×6. Write the 4-table up to ×10.

LONG-ANSWER (model). Q: Explain two ways to find 6 × 7 and check they agree. Model: (i) Repeated addition: 7+7+7+7+7+7 = 42. (ii) Break apart: 6×7 = 6×5 + 6×2 = 30+12 = 42. Both give 42, confirming the product.

HOTS. If 8×6=48, use it to find 8×7 without a table. (48+8=56.) A shop stacks apples in 9 rows of 9; is that more or fewer than 80? (81 — one more than 80.) Find a number that gives the same result whether you add it to itself or multiply it by 2 (any number — a+a = 2×a).

MISCONCEPTIONS. "×0 gives the number" → it gives 0. "3×4 and 3+4 are close" → 12 vs 7; different operations. "Order changes the product" → it doesn't (commutative). "Carrying is optional" → the carried ten must be added to the next column.

REVISION. Multiplication = repeated addition = arrays; commutative; ×0=0, ×1=same, ×10 adds a zero; column method with carrying; tables 2–10 patterns.
Formula/fact sheet: a×b = b×a; a×1=a; a×0=0; a×10 → append 0.

ADAPTIVE — "multiplication". L1: "Multiplication is a quick way to add the same number many times." L2: "4×6 means four groups of six; add 6 four times to get 24, or picture 4 rows of 6 dots." L3: "Multiplication is a binary operation on whole numbers giving the cardinality of a×b arrays; it is commutative, associative, and distributes over addition." L4: "Like stamping the same shape in a grid — 4 rows, 6 stamps each — you count all stamps at once instead of one by one." L5: "At a party, 6 tables each seat 8 people — 6×8=48 guests, found instantly instead of counting every chair."

CHAPTER G3M-B — Division & Fair Sharing ("Fair Share") — FULL APPARATUS
METADATA. Grade 3 · NCERT · Maths · Prereq: multiplication · Medium · Objectives: understand division as equal sharing and as equal grouping; relate division to multiplication (inverse); divide within tables; understand remainder; introduce the unit-fraction idea of a "share."

TEACHING MATERIAL.
Two meanings. (i) Sharing: 12 sweets shared equally among 4 children → 3 each (12 ÷ 4 = 3). (ii) Grouping: 12 sweets put into groups of 4 → 3 groups (12 ÷ 4 = 3). Both give the same answer.
Division undoes multiplication. Because 4 × 3 = 12, we have 12 ÷ 4 = 3 and 12 ÷ 3 = 4. Every "fact family" links two multiplications and two divisions.
Remainder. Not every number shares evenly. 13 ÷ 4 = 3 with remainder 1 (3 each, 1 left over). The remainder is always smaller than the divisor.
Terms. dividend ÷ divisor = quotient (remainder). In 13 ÷ 4: dividend 13, divisor 4, quotient 3, remainder 1.
Fraction seed. Sharing 1 chapati between 2 = one-half (½) each — the beginning of fractions.

Worked examples.
1. 20 ÷ 5 = 4 (5×4=20). 2. 17 ÷ 3 = 5 r 2 (3×5=15, 17−15=2). 3. Word: "24 laddoos, 6 boxes, equal — how many per box?" → 24 ÷ 6 = 4.

Visual/diagram description. 12 circles being dealt one-by-one into 4 boxes until each box holds 3 — showing equal sharing.

DEFINITIONS (10). division, share equally, group equally, dividend, divisor, quotient, remainder, inverse operation, fact family, unit fraction (½, ⅓).

Q&A. Easy: 15 ÷ 3? (5.) 10 ÷ 2? (5.) Medium: 19 ÷ 4? (4 r 3.) Write the fact family of 6, 4, 24. (4×6=24, 6×4=24, 24÷4=6, 24÷6=4.) Application: 28 students in 4 equal rows — per row? (7.) Share 25 pens among 5 → each? (5.) Reasoning: Why can't the remainder be 4 when dividing by 4? (It would make another full group.)

MCQs (12, abridged). 20÷4=5; 18÷3=6; 13÷4=3 r 1; 24÷6=4; inverse of ×: ÷; remainder of 22÷5=2; 30÷5=6; 0÷7=0; 9÷1=9; share 12/3=4; dividend in 15÷5: 15; quotient of 35÷7: 5.

T/F (10). Division is repeated subtraction of equal groups T. 12÷4 and 4÷12 are equal F. Remainder can equal the divisor F. 24÷6=4 because 6×4=24 T. 0÷5=0 T. 5÷0 has a normal answer F (undefined). Sharing and grouping give different answers F. Quotient is the answer of division T. Every remainder is less than the divisor T. 9÷1=9 T.

FILL BLANKS (10). 16÷=4 (4). 21÷3= (7). 13÷4=3 r  (1). 24÷=6 (4). ÷5=6 (30). 0÷9= (0). 18÷2= (9). fact family of 3,7,21: 21÷7= (3). 45÷9= (5). remainder of 20÷6= (2).

SHORT/LONG. Short: Divide 29 by 6, state quotient and remainder. Long (model): "Explain, with sharing and grouping, why 15 ÷ 3 = 5." Model — Sharing: deal 15 among 3 people → 5 each. Grouping: make groups of 3 from 15 → 5 groups. Same answer 5; and 3×5=15 confirms it.

HOTS. Find a number that leaves remainder 2 when divided by 5 and remainder 0 when divided by 3 (e.g., 12: 12÷5=2 r2, 12÷3=4 r0). Why is 7÷0 impossible? (You can't make groups of nothing; nothing shared can total 7.)

MISCONCEPTIONS. "Division is commutative" → it is not (12÷4≠4÷12). "Remainder can be any size" → it must be < divisor. "Dividing by 0 gives 0" → it is undefined.

REVISION. Two meanings (share/group); division inverts multiplication; fact families; remainder < divisor; ÷1 unchanged, ÷ itself =1, 0÷n=0, n÷0 undefined.

ADAPTIVE — "remainder". L1: "The remainder is what's left over when things can't be shared equally." L2: "Share 13 toffees among 4 kids: each gets 3 (that's 12) and 1 toffee is left — the remainder is 1." L3: "In Euclidean division a = bq + r with 0 ≤ r < b; r is the residue." L4: "Like seating people at tables of 4 — if one extra person arrives, they're the 'leftover' who can't fill a new table alone." L5: "Packing 22 eggs into cartons of 6: you fill 3 cartons and 4 eggs remain uncartoned — remainder 4."

CHAPTER G3E-A — Getting to Know Plants — FULL APPARATUS (anchor)
METADATA. Grade 3 · NCERT · EVS · Unit: Life Around Us · Prereq: Grade 1–2 plant awareness · Easy–Medium · Objectives: name plant parts and their functions; classify plants (trees/shrubs/herbs/climbers/creepers); understand what plants need; know plants as food/oxygen sources; describe seed→plant growth.

TEACHING MATERIAL.
Parts and functions. Root anchors the plant and absorbs water/minerals from soil. Stem holds the plant up and carries water and food between roots and leaves. Leaf makes food using sunlight (photosynthesis — made formal later) and lets the plant "breathe." Flower is the plant's reproductive part; it becomes fruit. Fruit protects and carries seeds, which grow into new plants.
Types of plants. Trees — tall, thick woody stems (mango, neem). Shrubs — medium, bushy, woody near base (rose, hibiscus). Herbs — small, soft stems (coriander, mint). Climbers — weak stems needing support (grapevine). Creepers — spread along the ground (pumpkin).
Needs. Air, water, sunlight, soil (nutrients) and space. Remove any, and the plant weakens.
Uses. Food (grains, fruits, vegetables), oxygen, wood, medicine, shade, and holding soil together.
Growth. A seed sprouts (germination) → tiny root and shoot → leaves → mature plant → flowers → fruit/seeds → new plants (life cycle).

Table — plant types.
 Type  |  Stem  |  Height  |  Example 
------------------------------
 Tree  |  thick, woody  |  tall  |  mango 
 Shrub  |  woody, bushy  |  medium  |  rose 
 Herb  |  soft, green  |  short  |  mint 
 Climber  |  weak, needs support  |  varies  |  grapevine 
 Creeper  |  weak, along ground  |  low  |  pumpkin 
Visual/diagram description. A labelled plant: roots underground, stem rising, leaves branching, a flower turning into a fruit with seeds inside.

DEFINITIONS (10). root, stem, leaf, flower, fruit, seed, germination, herb, shrub, climber.

Q&A. Easy: Which part makes food? (Leaf.) Which anchors the plant? (Root.) Medium: Give an example of a creeper (pumpkin). What does a stem carry? (Water and food.) Application: A plant kept in a dark cupboard — what happens? (It becomes weak/yellow; no sunlight to make food.) Reasoning: Why do desert plants have deep/spread roots? (To reach and gather scarce water.)

CHAPTER G4M-A — Fractions (Introduction) — FULL APPARATUS (anchor)
METADATA. Grade 4 · NCERT · Maths · Prereq: division/sharing (G3) · Medium · Objectives: understand a fraction as equal parts of a whole/collection; read/write fractions; identify numerator/denominator; compare simple fractions; find equivalent fractions; recognise halves/thirds/quarters; add/subtract like fractions.

TEACHING MATERIAL.
What is a fraction? When a whole is divided into equal parts, a fraction names some of those parts. In ¾: the denominator (4) tells how many equal parts the whole is cut into; the numerator (3) tells how many are taken. Parts must be equal — three unequal pieces are not thirds.
Fractions of a collection. Half of 8 apples = 4; a quarter of 12 = 3. "Fraction of a set" links to division.
Equivalent fractions. Different fractions can name the same amount: ½ = 2/4 = 3/6. Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number keeps the value.
Comparing. With the same denominator, the bigger numerator is bigger (3/5 > 2/5). With the same numerator, the smaller denominator is bigger (½ > ⅓, because halves are larger pieces than thirds).
Add/subtract like fractions. Same denominator: add/subtract numerators, keep the denominator (2/7 + 3/7 = 5/7).
Types. Proper fraction (numerator < denominator, e.g., ¾), whole = 4/4 = 1.

Worked examples.
1. Shade 3 of 4 equal parts → ¾. 2. ½ of 10 = 5. 3. Is ½ = 3/6? Yes (×3 top and bottom). 4. 4/9 + 2/9 = 6/9.

Visual/diagram description. A circle cut into 4 equal parts with 3 shaded = ¾; beside it a bar split into 2 (½) aligned with a bar split into 4 showing 2/4 covering the same length.

DEFINITIONS (10). fraction, whole, equal parts, numerator, denominator, proper fraction, unit fraction, equivalent fractions, like fractions, half/third/quarter.

Q&A. Easy: Write "three out of five" as a fraction (3/5). What is the denominator in ⅜? (8.) Medium: Which is bigger, ½ or ⅓? (½.) ½ of 14? (7.) Difficult: Find a fraction equal to ⅔ with denominator 9 (6/9). 5/8 − 2/8? (3/8.) Application: A pizza is cut into 8 slices; you eat 3 — what fraction is left? (5/8.) Reasoning: Why is ⅕ smaller than ¼? (Cutting into more parts makes each part smaller.)

MCQs (12, abridged). ¾ numerator: 3; denominator of 2/7: 7; ½ = 2/4; bigger: ½ or ⅓ → ½; ⅓ of 9: 3; 1/5+2/5: 3/5; whole as fraction of fourths: 4/4; equal parts needed for thirds: 3; ½ of 20: 10; 6/8−2/8: 4/8; proper fraction: 3/5; ¼ of 12: 3.

SHORT/LONG. Short: Compare 3/7 and 5/7; explain. Long (model): "Explain equivalent fractions with ½." Model — ½ means 1 of 2 equal parts. If each part is split again into 2, we get 2 of 4 parts (2/4) covering the same amount; split into 3 → 3/6. Multiplying top and bottom by the same number gives equivalent fractions: ½ = 2/4 = 3/6, all equal in value.

REVISION. Fraction = equal parts; numerator/denominator; equivalent fractions (×same); compare (same denom → numerator; same numerator → denom); add/subtract like fractions.
Fact sheet: ½=2/4=3/6…; a/b + c/b = (a+c)/b; whole = n/n = 1.

CHAPTER G5M-A — Decimals (Introduction) — FULL APPARATUS (anchor)
METADATA. Grade 5 · NCERT · Maths · Prereq: fractions, place value · Medium · Objectives: understand decimals as tenths/hundredths; read/write decimals; place value after the point; relate fractions↔decimals; compare/order; add/subtract decimals; use in money and measurement.

TEACHING MATERIAL.
Why decimals? Some quantities fall between whole numbers — ₹2 and 50 paise, or 1 metre 5 cm. Decimals extend place value to the right of a decimal point for parts smaller than one.
Place value after the point. First place = tenths (1/10 = 0.1), second = hundredths (1/100 = 0.01). So 0.7 = 7/10; 0.05 = 5/100; 0.35 = 35/100 = 3 tenths + 5 hundredths.
Fraction ↔ decimal. ½ = 5/10 = 0.5; ¼ = 25/100 = 0.25; 3/10 = 0.3.
Reading. 2.35 = "two point three five" = 2 wholes + 3 tenths + 5 hundredths.
Comparing. Line up decimal points; compare place by place from the left. 0.7 > 0.65 (7 tenths > 6 tenths). Adding zeros to the right doesn't change value (0.7 = 0.70).
Add/subtract. Line up the decimal points (and places) and add/subtract as usual, keeping the point aligned. 2.35 + 1.4 = 2.35 + 1.40 = 3.75.
Money & measure. ₹2.50 = 2 rupees 50 paise; 1.05 m = 1 m 5 cm.

Worked examples.
1. Write 7/10 and 9/100 as decimals → 0.7 and 0.09. 2. 0.6 + 0.25 = 0.85. 3. 3.5 − 1.75 = 1.75. 4. Compare 0.4 and 0.40 → equal.

Visual/diagram description. A 10×10 grid = 1 whole; shading 35 squares shows 0.35 = 35/100; a single column shaded = 0.1 (a tenth).

DEFINITIONS (10). decimal, decimal point, tenths, hundredths, decimal place, whole part, fractional part, equivalent decimal, place-value alignment, terminating decimal.

Q&A. Easy: Write 3/10 as a decimal (0.3). Read 0.9 (nine tenths). Medium: 0.5 + 0.3 (0.8). Which is bigger, 0.7 or 0.07? (0.7.) Difficult: 4.2 − 1.65 (2.55). Convert ¼ to a decimal (0.25). Application: A pen costs ₹12.50 and a book ₹35.75 — total? (₹48.25.) Reasoning: Why is 0.5 = 0.50? (Adding a zero in the hundredths place adds nothing; both are five tenths.)

MCQs (12, abridged). 0.1 = 1/10; 7/10 = 0.7; tenths digit of 3.46: 4; 0.6+0.2: 0.8; bigger: 0.9 or 0.19 → 0.9; ¼ = 0.25; 0.05 = 5/100; 2.5−1.5: 1.0; ₹0.50 = 50 paise; 0.3+0.07: 0.37; equal to 0.4: 0.40; hundredths in 0.28: 8.

T/F (10). 0.5 = 5/10 T. 0.7 < 0.07 F. 0.4 = 0.40 T. The first place after the point is hundredths F (tenths). ¼ = 0.25 T. To add decimals, line up the points T. 1.2 + 0.8 = 2.0 T. 0.9 has 9 hundredths F (9 tenths). Money uses two decimal places for paise T. 3/10 = 0.03 F (0.3).

MISCONCEPTIONS. "More digits after the point = bigger" → false (0.19 < 0.7). "0.5 and 0.05 are the same" → tenths vs hundredths. "Ignore the point when adding" → align points first.
